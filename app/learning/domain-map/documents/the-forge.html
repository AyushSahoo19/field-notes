Honest calibration

Read this before anything else.

You want this fast. Here is the truthful version, because a plan built on a fantasy timeline is a plan abandoned in month three.

Genuine depth across the territory in this document costs roughly 2,500–3,500 hours of focused, difficult work. Not watching. Not reading. Building, failing, debugging, rebuilding.

The phases below sum to 178 phase-weeks if run strictly one after another. You will not run them strictly one after another — parallel tracks overlap, and your existing experience lets you compress several phases hard. The realistic figure is 30–36 months at 15–20 hrs/week. 02

The learning OS

The AI Rule

AI is a force multiplier on understanding you already have, and a substitute for understanding you don't. The second use is what quietly destroys learners. Split every task explicitly.

RED

No AI generation. Ever.

  • The core artifact of any phase project. If the phase says 'write an allocator', you write every line.
  • Any algorithm or data structure you are meeting for the first time.
  • The first 30 minutes of any debugging session. Read the trace. Form a hypothesis. Then ask.
  • Anything you would be embarrassed not to reproduce at a whiteboard.
AMBER

AI as Socratic tutor, never as author.

  • "Explain why this mmap call returns EINVAL" — yes. "Fix my mmap call" — no.
  • "Give me three designs for this and what each trades off" — yes.
  • "Quiz me on page-table walks until I get five right in a row" — one of the highest-value uses available.
  • Reviewing your working code afterwards: "what would a kernel maintainer flag here?"
GREEN

Full use, no guilt.

  • Boilerplate, build files, CI config, Dockerfiles, glue scripts.
  • Frontend scaffolding on projects that are not the learning target.
  • Documentation, README polish, blog editing.
  • Any domain where you have already demonstrated mastery.

The test. Delete the AI output. Could you reproduce it from your own understanding in 48 hours? If no, it was Red Zone and you cheated yourself.

The Build-First Loop

Never read a book cover to cover then build. That fails. Every module runs this loop.

StepTimeWhat you do
Provoke1–2 hrsAttempt the project BEFORE studying. Fail. Feel precisely where you are stuck. This is the step everyone skips and the one that makes the reading stick.
Skim2 hrsRead the relevant chapter fast — headings, figures, summary only. You are looking for the shape of the answer, not the answer.
Build60–70% of module timeWrite it. Get it working badly. Working-and-ugly beats elegant-and-theoretical.
Deepen20%Now read the chapter properly. You will absorb four times as much because you have hooks to hang it on.
Rebuild10%Extend the project with what you now know, or rewrite it in a second language.
Teach1–2 hrsPublish the write-up. The module is not finished until it is public.

Three-Pass Reading

PassTimeMethod
Pass 1 — Map30 min / chapterHeadings, intro, summary, every figure. Write down the three questions the chapter answers. Do not read prose.
Pass 2 — Working read2–4 hrsRead properly. Do the in-chapter exercises only. Take notes as QUESTIONS, not statements. 'How is a TLB miss resolved?' not 'TLBs cache page table entries.'
Pass 3 — Adversarial1 hr, one week laterBook closed. Answer your own questions from memory. Whatever you cannot answer is your real gap — return only to that section.

Reading a research paper — Keshav's method

PassTimeMethod
Pass 15–10 minTitle, abstract, intro, headings, conclusion, familiar references. Decide: continue or drop. Drop 80%.
Pass 21 hrWhole paper, skip proofs. Understand every figure. Mark references to chase.
Pass 34–5 hrsVirtually re-implement. Reconstruct the argument and attack every assumption. Do this for ~10 papers a year, not 100.

Proof of Work

Every module ends in a public artifact. Three reasons: writing exposes fake understanding instantly, it compounds into a portfolio that outperforms any résumé, and it is how the communities in the directory find you.

  • A repository per project — real README, an architecture section, 'what I got wrong', 'what I'd do differently'.
  • A blog on your own domain, static, no platform. One post per module. Target 70–90 posts across the program.
  • The format that works: "I built X. Here is the thing I got wrong for three days, and why." Debugging narratives are the most-read technical writing on the internet.
  • An interactive explainer per tier — a scheduler visualiser, a B+ tree animator, an attention visualiser. These teach you the material twice and are the single best distribution mechanism for a technical reputation.

Gates

A gate is not 'I finished the book.' It is a demonstrable capability, performed without notes. Fail a gate and you loop the phase. That is the mechanism working, not the plan failing.

  • Attempt the gate cold — no reference material, no AI, no earlier code open.
  • Time-box it. If the gate says four hours, four hours is part of the test.
  • Record yourself or write the attempt up. You will lie to yourself about how it went; the recording will not.
  • Fail twice in a row → the phase before it was weak. Go back one phase, not zero.

The Stuck Protocol

When you are blocked, follow this clock. Do not skip to the end.

ElapsedDo this
0–15 minRead the actual error. All of it. Then re-read the code you changed most recently.
15–30 minReduce to a minimal reproduction. Half of all bugs die here.
30–60 minInstrument. Print, log, gdb, strace, perf. Get evidence, not theories.
60–90 minExplain the problem out loud to something that cannot answer. Write the explanation down.
90 minNOW ask — an AI, a forum, a person. Include what you tried and what you observed, never just the error.
4+ hrsStop. Sleep on it. The overnight solve rate on hard systems bugs is remarkably high.
2 daysYou are missing a prerequisite, not a detail. Identify which earlier module it lives in and go back.

Why this order

  • P01 and P02 before P03 — you cannot reason about C without knowing what a machine does, and you cannot reason about correctness without proof technique.
  • P04 runs early and long — algorithmic maturity is the slowest-maturing skill here and cannot be crammed.
  • P05 → P06 is a deliberate escalation — use the operating system, then become it.
  • P09 sits mid-programme — compiler concepts (IR, SSA, graph rewriting) are the secret prerequisite for P16's ML compiler work.
  • P11 requires P07 and P10 — distributed systems without networking and storage is hand-waving.
  • P12 before P16 — modern AI is a performance engineering discipline wearing an ML hat.
  • Tier IV last — you rebuild your existing domain on solid ground rather than beside it.
  • P17 late — design principles are platitudes until you have built enough to have felt the pain they prevent.

P01Stratum I · Foundations

Discrete Mathematics & Theory of Computation

The formal ground under everything. What computation is, and what it cannot do.

Weeks8 Hours~140 Difficulty NeedsP00 UnlocksP04 P09 Projects5

Why this phase exists

This is the phase most self-taught engineers skip, and skipping it is precisely why they plateau. Without proof technique you cannot reason about correctness — you can only test and hope. Without complexity theory you cannot tell an impossible problem from a hard one, and you will waste months. Without automata you will find regular expressions, parsers, protocol state machines, and tokenizers permanently mysterious. This is also the phase that most directly separates a computer scientist from a programmer, which is what you said you want.

What you will be able to doYou can write and check a rigorous proof, reason about asymptotics from first principles, model any protocol or parser as a state machine, and correctly identify when a problem is undecidable or NP-hard rather than merely difficult.
Step 0 · The warm-up

Before opening any book: try to prove that the square root of 2 is irrational, and try to argue why no program can decide whether an arbitrary program halts. You will probably fail at both. Note exactly where your reasoning stops being rigorous — that gap is what this phase closes.

Rhythm

This phase inverts the usual build/read ratio: roughly 60% reading and problem sets, 40% building. That is correct here and only here. Do problem sets on paper, by hand. Typing them defeats the purpose.

Traps in this phase
  • Reading proofs instead of writing them. Reading a proof produces the feeling of understanding and none of the substance. Write 20 proofs minimum this phase.
  • Treating the pumping lemma as a party trick. It is the first time you will prove something is IMPOSSIBLE, which is a genuinely new mode of thought.
  • Skipping to Sipser because automata feel more 'useful'. Without Weeks 1–2 you will not be able to follow his proofs.
  • Believing NP-hard means 'slow'. It means something precise, and confusing the two will cost you real engineering decisions later.

CompressionCompress to 5 weeks: keep Weeks 1–2 (proofs) and Weeks 6–8 (automata/complexity) at full length; halve the counting and probability weeks, since you will rebuild probability properly in P13.

P02Stratum I · Foundations

Digital Logic & Computer Architecture

From a single NAND gate to a working CPU. The end of magic.

Weeks8 Hours~145 Difficulty NeedsP00 UnlocksP03 P12 Projects5

Why this phase exists

You cannot reason about performance, concurrency, or memory without an accurate mental model of the machine — and almost every self-taught engineer is running on a model that is wrong in ways they cannot see. Cache behaviour, false sharing, branch misprediction, memory ordering, and every GPU optimisation in P12 are consequences of architecture. This is also the fastest known cure for the feeling that computers are magic: build one from gates and the feeling never returns.

What you will be able to doYou have designed a CPU from NAND gates and written its assembler. You can read x86-64 disassembly, predict cache behaviour from a loop's memory access pattern, and explain why two algorithmically identical programs differ 10× in speed.
Step 0 · The warm-up

Write a C program with two loops that touch the same array — one row-major, one column-major over a 2D array. Time both. The difference will be large and you will not be able to explain it. Write down your current best guess; you will read it back in Week 7 and wince.

P03Stratum I · Foundations

C & The Machine Model

The language that sits closest to the metal, and the mental model it gives you.

Weeks10 Hours~180 Difficulty NeedsP02 UnlocksP04 P05 P09 Projects6

Why this phase exists

Every abstraction you will ever debug resolves downward into pointers, memory layout, and process state. A Python GIL stall, a CUDA out-of-memory, a container's memory limit, a PyTorch dataloader deadlock — all of them are C-level phenomena wearing a costume. Without this phase you pattern-match symptoms forever. With it you reason from mechanism. It is the highest-leverage 180 hours in the document.

What you will be able to doYou write non-trivial C confidently and safely, understand undefined behaviour as a real hazard rather than trivia, can implement a memory allocator, and have built the fundamental Unix abstractions yourself.
Step 0 · The warm-up

Write a program that reads a file into memory, reverses every line in place, and writes it back — in C, with no library beyond read/write/malloc. Do it before studying anything. Count the segfaults. That number is your baseline and it will be embarrassing.

Correct first, then fast. The slowest-maturing skill in this document.

Why this phase exists

Two distinct skills get confused under this heading. Algorithmic DESIGN is inventing a solution to a problem nobody has handed you. Interview problem-solving is recognising a known pattern under time pressure. You need both and they train differently. This phase runs early and long because algorithmic maturity takes 18+ months to develop and cannot be crammed — which is exactly why it is the one phase in this document you must not compress.

Step 0 · The warm-up

Open CSES and attempt five problems from the 'Introductory Problems' set, timed, before studying anything. Then attempt one from 'Dynamic Programming'. The gap between how those two feel is the shape of this phase.

Weeks8 Hours~150 Difficulty NeedsP05 UnlocksP08 P11 Projects6

Why this phase exists

Everything you build after this is networked, and 'how the internet works' is a question you can only really answer by implementing the stack. Implementing TCP in userspace is the single most clarifying networking exercise in existence: retransmission, flow control, and congestion control stop being vocabulary and become code you had to make work against a real remote host.

Why this phase exists

You are building systems that will hold other people's data and, increasingly, AI systems that accept untrusted input as instructions. Security is not a phase you bolt on — it is a mode of reasoning, and the only reliable way to acquire it is to break things yourself. Traps in this phase
  • Optimising without profiling. The bottleneck is essentially never where you think it is. This is the most reliable law in performance engineering.
  • Micro-optimising before checking the algorithm. A better algorithm beats a better constant factor almost every time — check O() first, always.
  • Benchmarking without controlling for frequency scaling, cache warm-up, and variance. Most published benchmarks are wrong for exactly these reasons.
  • Learning CUDA without a GPU. Use Colab, Kaggle, Lightning, or a cheap cloud instance — but you must have real hardware to profile against. Nsight Compute on a real GPU is not optional.

5
Maintain your own

Ongoing

Ultimately more valuable than contributing to others. One genuinely useful library, maintained well, teaches more about software than fifty merged PRs.

Where Your own repository.

How to make a first contribution that lands

  1. Use the project for a month first. Contributing to software you do not use produces bad patches.
  2. Read CONTRIBUTING.md completely. Then read it again.
  3. Lurk in the issue tracker and chat for two weeks before speaking.
  4. Pick an issue nobody is on. Comment your intent and your proposed approach before writing code.
  5. Small PR. One concern. Tests included. A commit message explaining why, not what.
  6. Take review feedback without ego. Iterate quickly.
  7. Then do it again in the same project. Repeated contribution to one project builds far more than scattered PRs across ten.

How this fails

Read this section every three months. These are the specific ways serious, capable people wreck plans like this one.

01
Tutorial hell with better production values

Looks likeYou watch the CMU lectures, feel enlightened, never do the labs.

The fixIf you cannot produce a working artifact from a module, you did not learn it. Labs first, lectures when stuck.

02
AI-assisted illusion of competence

Looks likeYou 'build' the allocator in two hours with help, it works, you move on. Six months later you cannot write free().

03
Breadth stampede

Looks likeThis document names roughly 250 resources. Touching all of them produces zero depth.

The fixEach phase has ONE primary book and ONE primary project. Everything else is optional. Ship the primary.

04
The perpetual restart

Looks likeMonth four, you find a better roadmap, you start over. This is the most common failure of all.

The fixCommit for twelve months without switching. Revise at month 12, not month 4.

05
Skipping gates

Looks likeYou 'sort of get' P05, move to P06, and your kernel work becomes copy-paste on a shaky foundation.

The fixThe gates are the plan. Fail one, loop the phase.

06
Neglecting the write-up

Looks likeYou build it, don't publish it, and in eight months you cannot remember how it worked — and nobody knows you did it.

07
Optimising for the résumé instead of the skill

Looks likeYou do the visible things (contest ratings, certificates) and skip the invisible ones (reading kernel source for six hours).
